WordPress website maintenance cost is the line most business owners forget when they budget for a new site. Then a plugin update breaks the checkout, or the site gets hacked, and the emergency fix costs more than a year of maintenance would have.

Here is what maintenance involves, what it typically costs, and what a good plan should include.

Why WordPress Needs Maintenance

WordPress core, your theme, and every plugin receive regular updates. Many fix security vulnerabilities. Skipping updates leaves known holes open, and updating blindly can break layouts or features. Maintenance is the process of doing it safely, plus backups and monitoring.

Typical WordPress Maintenance Costs

These are common monthly ranges for freelance maintenance, in US dollars:

  • Basic, around $50 to $100 per month: updates, backups, uptime and security monitoring for a small business site.
  • Standard, around $100 to $250 per month: everything in basic, plus a set number of content changes, speed checks, and priority support.
  • WooCommerce and larger sites, $250 and up: order-critical monitoring, checkout testing after updates, and more development time.

The right level depends on how important the site is to your revenue and how often you need changes.

What a Good Maintenance Plan Includes

1. Safe Updates

Core, theme, and plugin updates, applied after a fresh backup, ideally tested on a staging copy first for critical sites. After updating, key pages, forms, and checkout are checked.

2. Automatic Off-Site Backups

I set up scheduled backups with tools like UpdraftPlus, or the host's own backup system, sent to off-site storage like Google Drive or Dropbox. Backups stored only on the same server are not enough, because if the server fails, the backups go with it. A backup is only useful if restoring it works, so restores should be tested.

3. Security Hardening and Monitoring

  • A firewall and malware scanner, such as Wordfence, or protection at the server or CDN level like Cloudflare.
  • Limited login attempts and two-factor authentication for admins.
  • Removing unused plugins and themes.
  • Strong passwords and correct user roles.
  • Monitoring for malware and suspicious file changes.

4. Spam Protection

Contact forms and WooCommerce registrations attract spam bots. Honeypot fields, Cloudflare Turnstile or reCAPTCHA, and spam filters on WPForms or Elementor forms keep your inbox clean without annoying real visitors.

5. Speed Monitoring

Sites slow down over time as content and plugins grow. Regular checks of PageSpeed and Core Web Vitals, cache configuration, and image optimization keep them fast.

6. Uptime Monitoring

An uptime monitor alerts me when the site goes down, often before the client notices.

7. Small Content and Design Changes

Updating prices, adding a page, changing a banner. Including a few of these in the plan is usually cheaper than paying per request.

DIY Maintenance vs Hiring a Developer

You can handle updates yourself on a simple site if you keep reliable backups and check the site after each update. Hire a developer when the site generates revenue, has custom code, or runs WooCommerce, where a broken update costs real money.

Frequently Asked Questions

How often should WordPress be updated?

Check for updates at least weekly. Apply security updates as soon as possible, after a backup.

Can a hacked WordPress site be fixed?

Usually, yes: malware is removed, vulnerabilities are closed, passwords are reset, and the site is restored from a clean backup if needed. Prevention is still cheaper.

Is maintenance included in the website build price?

Usually not. Most developers include a short support period after launch, then offer a monthly plan.
